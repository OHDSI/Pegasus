Acute liver injury is a sudden-onset hepatocellular insult occurring in a liver with no pre-existing chronic disease, manifesting within six months of onset and encompassing the full spectrum of biochemical or histologic injury patterns—hepatocellular, mixed, and cholestatic—and all severities, from asymptomatic transaminase elevation to fulminant hepatic failure with coagulopathy and hepatic encephalopathy. The concept includes injury arising from any etiology, such as drugs, toxins, viruses, ischemia or hypoxia, autoimmune reactions, metabolic or vascular disorders, infiltrative processes, or idiopathic causes, without restriction by patient age, life stage, or exposure context, and without limitation on laterality or lobar distribution. It requires confirmed clinical, laboratory, or histopathologic evidence of acute hepatocyte damage, and explicitly excludes patients with established chronic liver diseases (thus omitting acute-on-chronic liver injury), purely extrahepatic obstructive cholestasis lacking hepatocellular injury, remote or resolved past liver injury, and cases merely suspected or ultimately ruled out.
